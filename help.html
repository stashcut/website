Stashcut Help

Basics

Copy the same way as always. Launching Stashcut does not clear the system clipboard.

Paste the same way as always.

Press the Main Hotkey (⇧+⌘+v by default) to access your clipboard history. Press the Search hotkey (⇧+⌘+? by default) to search it. Or click the Stashcut mark in the menu bar, the little card between two side bars.

The Bezel

The Bezel is what you see when you press the Main Hotkey. Use Left and Right to step through your history, and Up and Down to choose a stack's front layer. The home-row aliases work too. Every other layer stands above the front card with its own named tab, including Screenshot and Text on an image clipping. Click a tab or an exposed back to select it. The front tab leaves the selection unchanged.

Sticky bezel is on by default, so there is no need to hold the command key. When you find something you want to paste, press Return and it is pasted while the Bezel closes automatically. If you don't want to paste, just press the Esc key. Space or right-click pins the Bezel so it stays until you paste, Esc, or click away.

Bezel Keystrokes

Here's a complete list of keys you can use in the Bezel.

→: Move to the next, older stack in your clipboard history.

←: Move to the previous, newer stack in your clipboard history.

↑: Bring the next layer to the front, in the order the layers were made. Every other layer stands above the card with its own named tab. Up from the last layer wraps to Original (Screenshot on an image).

↓: Bring the previous layer to the front. Up and Down wrap around the stack in creation order and never change stacks. You can also click a tab or an exposed card body above the front card to select its layer directly.

The implicit home-row aliases are h/l for stacks and k/j for layers. The key hints show arrows only.

↩: Paste the clipboard history item currently displayed, closing the Bezel.

esc: Close the bezel without pasting. If favorites were selected, returns to the main list.

fn+↩ (Enter): Move the clipboard history item currently displayed to the front of the history, closing the Bezel.

⌘+,: Display the Stashcut preferences panel.

home: Move to the most recent item in your clipboard history.

end: Move to the least recent item in your clipboard history.

page down: Move backward ten positions in your clipboard history.

page up: Move forward ten positions in your clipboard history.

⌫ or ⌦: Delete the front AI result and reveal the preceding layer. On Original, delete the whole stack. The bezel stays open while there are clippings left.

1 through 9 and 0: Move to the clipboard history position indicated by the number, or position 10 for zero.

s or ⇧+s: Save the clipboard history item currently displayed to a file, closing the Bezel. Delete the item from the clipboard history if Shift was held. Clipping is saved to a file on the Desktop, akin to how Screen Shots are saved by OS X. e.g. "Desktop/Clipping 2015-07-15 at 08.35.10.txt"

f: Switch between the main clipboard history store and the favorites store. The favorites store appears in the Bezel and works in the same way as the main clipboard history store, aside from having a yellow tint to the Bezel.

⇧+f: Move the whole current stack to favorites, keeping its original, results and front layer, and close the Bezel.

r: Reveal the value behind a 1Password credential card. Does nothing on any other clipping, which is why the bezel's key hint line only lists it while you are on a credential. See Keeping credentials in 1Password instead below.

t: On a screenshot, turn the card over to the text read out of it, and back. Pasting pastes whichever side is showing.

z: On a screenshot, show it full screen. esc comes back to the bezel.

b: On a clipping with one link in it, open the link in your default browser, closing the Bezel. Only http and https links open.

o: Send the clipping currently displayed to your Obsidian vault, closing the Bezel. Does nothing until you choose a folder in Preferences > Obsidian, which is why the key hint line only lists it once you have. See Sending a clipping to Obsidian below.

Menu Features

The Stashcut menu allows you to select from the most recent items in the main clipboard history store, clear all of them, merge them all into one entry, or access the preferences panel. You can also ⌥-click the menu icon to disable or reenable clipboard tracking, in case you are copying sensitive information such as passwords.

Reading the bezel

The bezel in the middle is the clipping you are on. Either side of it are the clippings next to it in your history, two on the left and two on the right, all the same size and fainter the further out they are. Left is more recent and right is older, the same direction Left and Right move, and pressing either slides the whole row along by one. When there is no card on one side there is nothing further that way: you are at the start or the end of your history.

Only the middle bezel takes your keys, so the application you are about to paste into keeps its focus. You can click a side card, though: it comes to the middle, the same as arrowing to it. Cards brighten under the mouse to show they will.

The bezel is as tall as the clipping it is showing and no taller, so a one-line clipping is a single bar rather than a line of text in the corner of a large empty panel. Stepping to a longer clipping grows it, in step with the row sliding. Bezel height limit in Preferences > Appearance is as far as it will grow; Bezel width is a fixed width, because the width is what decides where the cards beside it sit. Each card is sized to its own clipping in the same way, from a floor: a card never gets smaller than that floor, so the row keeps its shape, and a long clipping gets a taller card to hold it.

While the bezel is open the rest of the screen is dimmed, so the clipping you are choosing is the only lit thing in front of you. The dimming never takes a click either. How dark it goes is the Dim behind bezel slider in Preferences > Appearance; all the way left turns it off.

That dimming does not have to be plain black. Backdrop, just below the slider, is what the screen behind the bezel is filled with:

  • Dim is the plain black it has always been, and is what you get if you never touch this.
  • Solid colour uses the first Backdrop colour well.
  • Gradient runs from the first well to the second, top to bottom.
  • Image shows a picture of your own, chosen with Choose Image.... Image scaling decides how it meets your screen: Fill covers the whole screen and crops what does not fit, Fit shows all of the picture and fills what is left over with the backdrop colour, and Tile repeats it at its own size from the top left, which is what to use for a pattern.

The Dim behind bezel slider still means the same thing whichever you pick: it is how solid the backdrop is, so a colour or a picture is still see-through at 65% and your desktop still reads through it. On more than one display each screen gets its own complete copy of the picture rather than a slice of one stretched across both.

A picture you choose is copied into ~/Library/Application Support/Stashcut/ Backdrops, so moving or deleting the original afterwards does not break it. If that copy ever goes missing, the backdrop quietly falls back to plain dim.

Along the bottom of the bezel, either side of the counter, are the keys that work while it is open. Turn them off with Show key hints along the bottom of the bezel once you know them. The carousel is Show neighbouring clippings beside the bezel, on by default.

Keeping credentials out of your history

Stashcut recognises content a password manager marks as concealed. It also reads the content of what you copy and detects text that has the shape of a credential: private keys, JSON web tokens, database URLs with a password in them, lines like DB_PASSWORD=..., and API keys from the common issuers. This is what catches a key pasted out of a terminal, which no password manager ever marked.

Detection is on by default. Settings > Privacy controls whether detected values stay hidden in memory, are not kept, or are kept as copied.

Matching is deliberately cautious. It keys on issuer prefixes and lengths rather than on how random a string looks, so ordinary writing and source code are unaffected, and values that read as documentation are kept rather than discarded. The trade is that a credential of pure letters, with no digit in it, is not recognised.

When a credential is detected, a notice under the menu bar icon names what it looked like. The hidden clipping itself has a lock, a mask and its visible label. If you would rather not see it, run this in Terminal and restart Stashcut:

defaults write com.stashcut.app notifySkippedSecrets -bool NO

By default, detected credentials are kept as hidden, memory-only clippings. Their values are masked, never written to history on disk, and never sent to AI, search by meaning, Obsidian or coding agents. You can still paste them from the app or reveal them with r.

Settings > Privacy lets you choose to keep detected credentials hidden, not keep them, or keep them as copied. Hidden clippings are forgotten after 15 minutes, 1 hour, 8 hours or when Stashcut quits. The default deadline is one hour. Not a secret restores an incorrectly detected clipping to ordinary history.

1Password through a service account

Stashcut can reach 1Password two ways. The edition from stashcut.com uses 1Password's command line tool by default, with your whole account and 1Password's own approval prompt, as described next. Both editions can instead use a service account, and the Mac App Store edition can only use that: Apple's sandbox cannot run the command line tool. 1Password does not let a service account into your Private vault, so this route files credentials into a separate vault and reads them back from there. Once, on 1Password.com:

  1. Create a vault for Stashcut, or pick one you already set aside (any vault except Private and the default Shared one).
  2. Open Developer, then Service accounts, and create one with read and write access to that vault. Copy the token; 1Password shows it once, so save it in 1Password too.
  3. In Stashcut, open Preferences > 1Password. In the stashcut.com edition, set Connect through to Service account, a separate vault. Paste the token into the Token field and press Save. The field clears itself; the token lives in your keychain.
  4. Choose the vault from the list. From then on a detected credential can be filed there, and pasted back from the bezel without showing on screen.

Needs macOS 14 or later. You can switch back to the command line tool at any time in the stashcut.com edition; credentials already filed stay where they are.

Keeping credentials in 1Password instead

Hidden clippings last only for your session or chosen deadline. Preferences > 1Password offers a longer-term home: the credential goes into 1Password as a proper item, and Stashcut keeps a clipping that points at that item, so it is still in your history and can still be pasted from the bezel.

The switch is Offer to save detected credentials to 1Password and it is off by default. Turn it on, press Refresh, and pick a vault. Nothing is chosen for you, deliberately: if you have a work account and a personal one configured, both appear, each vault named with the account it belongs to, and a private credential landing in a work vault is exactly the mistake worth making impossible.

With it off, or with no vault chosen, or with the 1Password command line tool not installed, the credential follows your Privacy setting. By default it stays hidden in memory only. Saving a detected text credential to 1Password is optional; hidden screenshots are not offered for saving as credential items.

With it on, the notice under the menu bar icon gains a Save to 1Password button. Press it and the credential is filed; ignore it and it fades after a while, which means no, which is the old behaviour.

How long it waits. Panel stays for sets that: anywhere from 5 to 90 seconds, and 20 unless you change it. Twenty is long enough to finish the sentence you were typing and come back to it, and short enough not to become furniture.

Where it appears. Show offer as has two settings. Stashcut panel is the notice under the menu bar icon described here. macOS notification puts the same offer in a real notification instead, with the same Save to 1Password button on it, which is worth having if you keep missing the panel.

Banners or Alerts, which is the setting that actually matters. macOS decides how long one of its notifications stays on screen, not Stashcut, so the Panel stays for slider does not apply to it and goes grey when you choose the notification. The setting that does apply is in System Settings > Notifications > Stashcut, and there is a Notification Settings… button in Stashcut's pane that opens it:

  • Banners appear in the corner and take themselves away after about five seconds. If the thing you disliked was the offer vanishing before you could answer it, this will do the same.
  • Alerts stay until you dismiss them. The offer waits for you, which is almost certainly what you want here.

The Save to 1Password button is not drawn on a banner until you move the pointer over it or expand it, which is how macOS draws every notification with an action. An alert shows it straight away.

If notifications are turned off for Stashcut, or you have never answered the permission prompt, or macOS refuses to deliver one, the offer appears as the Stashcut panel instead and the 1Password pane says so. It is never silently dropped. Stashcut asks for permission to send notifications only when you pick the notification style, and never at launch.

How long the credential is kept while you decide. Only until the offer is answered or runs out. That is the slider's setting for the panel, and two minutes for a notification, because a notification can sit in Notification Center indefinitely and something has to bound it. After that Stashcut lets go of it: pressing Save on an offer that has expired tells you so and asks you to copy it again, rather than saving an empty item.

The notice will not interrupt what you are typing. It does not take the keyboard on its own, because detection fires exactly when you are in the middle of putting a key into a terminal or a form, and a panel that swallowed your next Return at that moment would be worse than the silence it replaces. Move the pointer onto it and it takes the keyboard, showing a return save esc dismiss line to say that it has; ↩ then saves and esc dismisses. Move the pointer off it and the keyboard goes straight back to whatever you were typing into. A pointer that happened to be resting where the notice opened does not count as having been moved onto it: move it away and back.

What is actually stored. The clipping holds the 1Password item's title and an op:// reference. Neither is a secret: someone reading Stashcut's preferences file learns which items exist, not what is in them. Stashcut never writes the credential to its own store, not even briefly.

Reading one back. A credential clipping shows as a card with a lock, the item title, the vault and account it lives in, and the value masked. Arrowing onto it and past it costs nothing and asks 1Password nothing, which is the point: opening the bezel must never raise a Touch ID sheet because a credential happened to be two cards away.

Press r on that card to show the value, and r again to put it away. That is the one keystroke that talks to 1Password, and the first press takes about a second, so the card says Revealing… while it waits. Showing it again after hiding it is instant: the value is held in memory for as long as that bezel is open and is gone when it closes, so hiding it is about what is on your screen rather than about what Stashcut is holding. It is never written anywhere. The key hint line along the bottom of the bezel lists r show/hide while a credential is on the card, and does not while an ordinary clipping is.

↩ pastes it, the same as on every other clipping, fetching it from 1Password first if you have not revealed it. When it has to fetch, the card shows Revealing… for about a second and the paste happens when the value arrives, so nothing is pasted early and nothing empty is pasted. The keypad enter puts the credential on the clipboard and closes the bezel without pasting it, which is what Enter does for an ordinary clipping too.

A pasted credential is put on the clipboard marked org.nspasteboard.ConcealedType, which other clipboard managers watch for and skip, and it is cleared from the clipboard about twelve seconds later if nothing else has replaced it by then.

If anything goes wrong at any point, including 1Password being locked, the card says so in one line and you can still arrow straight past it. There is never a dialog and never a dead end.

The first time. 1Password authorises the application that is calling it, not you, so the first time Stashcut runs op 1Password will show its own approval prompt for Stashcut. That is correct and it is asked once. Stashcut also needs 1Password's Settings > Developer > Integrate with 1Password CLI switched on, which is what lets op use the desktop app instead of asking for a session token.

Stashcut looks for op in /opt/homebrew/bin, /usr/local/bin and /usr/bin. If yours is somewhere else:

defaults write com.stashcut.app onePasswordCommandPath /path/to/op

Sending a clipping to Obsidian

Press o in the bezel and the clipping you are looking at is filed into your Obsidian vault as plain markdown. Send to Obsidian in the menu files the same clipping, which is the most recent one unless you left the bezel somewhere else. A notice appears under the menu bar icon naming the note it went to.

This is plain file writing and nothing else. No plugin, no URL scheme, nothing talking to a running Obsidian, so it works with Obsidian closed and does not care which version you have. Obsidian notices the new file itself.

Nothing is guessed, and nothing happens until you pick a folder. Go to Preferences > Obsidian and press Choose…. Everything you send goes into that one folder, so pick one you would be happy to find anything in: a vault usually holds work notes beside private ones, and a clipping filed into the wrong one cannot be unfiled. Clear puts it back to nothing, which is how you turn this off again. Until a folder is chosen, o says so and writes nothing, and the bezel's key hint line does not mention it.

Where it goes. Entries are appended to the daily note for the day the clipping was copied, 2026-09-20.md, inside the folder you picked, and the note is created if it is not there. Appending rather than one note per clipping is deliberate: a vault full of one-line fragments is a vault nobody reads. Filing something you copied three days ago puts it in that day's note, because the day and the time it was copied are the provenance that makes this worth more than a paste.

What an entry looks like. A heading of the time and the application, then the clipping itself inside a fenced code block:

## 14:32:07 Safari

```text
Meeting at four in the small room.
```

The heading is unique within the note, so you can link straight to it as [[2026-09-20#14:32:07 Safari]].

The fence is what makes this safe. A clipping is arbitrary text: it can hold triple backticks, a line starting with #, a line of three hyphens, or all three. The fence is always one backtick longer than the longest run of backticks anywhere in the clipping, so the clipping cannot close the block it is in, cannot change the note's structure, and cannot swallow whatever comes after it. Your text is written through exactly as it was copied, and Obsidian's copy button on a code block hands it straight back. The one exception is line endings: Windows CRLF and lone CR become LF, because a lone CR would render the whole clipping as a single line.

Screenshots are the reason to use this. An image clipping already carries the picture and the text Stashcut read out of it. Filing one copies the PNG into an attachments subfolder, embeds it, and writes the recognised text underneath, so your vault's own search finds the note by what the screenshot says. To put the pictures somewhere else:

defaults write com.stashcut.app obsidianAttachmentsFolder Files

That has to be one plain folder name. Anything with a slash, a colon, a leading dot or .. in it is ignored and attachments is used, so a hand-edited preference cannot write outside the folder you picked.

Credentials are never sent. A credential kept in 1Password is a reference, and the point of that is that the secret is not in a file Stashcut wrote. Pressing o on one says so and writes nothing.

When it cannot be written. A folder that has been renamed, deleted, left on a drive that is not connected, made read-only, or is momentarily unavailable in iCloud Drive gets one sentence naming it, and nothing is written at all. Nothing is ever half-written either: two clippings filed a second apart cannot land inside one another, and a write that fails part way through takes itself back out again, including removing a note it had just created.

Letting a coding agent read your history

Stashcut can be a tool that a coding agent calls. An agent such as opencode or Claude Code that can see what you copied in the last hour needs far less explaining to: the error message, the ticket number, the schema you pasted into a scratch file half an hour ago are all already in your history.

Off until you turn it on. Go to Preferences > Agents and tick Let coding agents read the clipboard history. Until you do, every request is refused with a message saying the feature is switched off, rather than an empty answer, so an agent can tell the difference between "nothing matched" and "not allowed".

Setting it up is one button. The same pane shows the exact path of the small program an agent runs, and has Configure opencode and Configure Claude beside it. Stashcut never opens or rewrites your configuration file. It runs that product's own command-line tool, the same opencode mcp add or claude mcp add you would type yourself, and nothing else.

Before anything runs you are shown the exact commands and can cancel. Stashcut first asks the tool to list its MCP servers; if stashcut is already there, nothing is changed and it says so. Otherwise it adds the entry, then lists again to confirm. If that second listing does not show stashcut, Stashcut says the configuration could not be verified rather than claiming it worked. In every case the tool's own output is shown to you verbatim.

When the CLI is not there. If the opencode or claude command cannot be found in its usual install locations or in the PATH Stashcut inherited, Stashcut does not guess at another executable and does not edit the file. It offers to copy a JSON block for you to merge yourself into the mcp section of ~/.config/opencode/opencode.json, or the mcpServers section of ~/.claude.json. The same offer appears if the command fails or the result cannot be verified. The block looks like this:

{
  "mcpServers": {
    "stashcut": {
      "args": [],
      "command": "/Applications/Stashcut.app/Contents/MacOS/stashcut-mcp"
    }
  }
}

Running Claude Code performs Claude's own migrations on ~/.claude.json. Those are Claude's, not Stashcut's, and Stashcut does not control them.

Three tools, all read-only. search finds clippings containing a piece of text, ignoring case and accents. get fetches one clipping by its position, where 0 is the most recent, the same numbering the bezel shows. recent lists the newest ones. There is nothing that writes to your history, deletes from it, or pastes.

What an agent is not given. Credentials you keep in 1Password are never handed over: not the value, not the op:// reference, not even the item's title. They are reported only as a count, so a gap in the numbering has an explanation. A clipping that trips the same check that asks before a risky paste, above, keeps its position and its time but its text is replaced by a line saying what was held back, for example [Stashcut withheld this clipping: it looks like a payment card number]. That is deliberate: an agent that cannot see a gap reasons around it and tells you something confident and wrong. Those clippings are also left out of searching altogether, so a query cannot be used to guess at their contents one character at a time. Favourites are not shared at all, and only the first 2000 characters of any clipping are searched or returned.

No port, no token. The program runs as a child process of the agent you started, reading its requests on a pipe. There is nothing listening on the network and nothing to manage. It works whether or not Stashcut itself is running, and it reads the history fresh on every request, so something you copy while the agent is working is there the next time it asks.

If Stashcut and the agent disagree about whether sharing is on, the agent is reading the preference at the moment of the request, so turning the switch off stops the next call rather than the one after a restart.

Asking before a risky paste

Stashcut asks before pasting something personal into an application whose job is sending things elsewhere. If the clipping looks like it holds a payment card number, a bank account number, a phone number, a long list of email addresses or a credential, and the paste is going to a web browser, a chat client or an AI chat application, a question appears first:

Paste a payment card number into Google Chrome?

Paste Anyway · Cancel · Don't Warn for Google Chrome

Cancel is the default, so a stray Return is the safe answer. Cancelling leaves your clipboard and your history exactly as they were. Don't Warn for ... pastes, and takes that application off the list for good.

This is a wider check than the one above. The check above decides what is worth storing; this one decides what is worth asking about before it leaves the machine, and it looks for things the other one never does: card numbers (validated with the Luhn check, so order numbers and long integers do not trip it), IBANs (validated with the mod-97 check), phone numbers in international form, and email addresses when there are five or more distinct ones. A single address never warns, because pasting one is an ordinary thing to do. It also warns about anything the store-time check would have caught, which matters if you turned that check off.

What Stashcut can and cannot see here. It knows which application you are pasting into. It does not know which site is loaded in it, and does not try to find out: reading the browser's current page means driving the browser, which is a much larger permission than a clipboard manager has any business asking for. So the guard works at the level of the application. It will ask about a card number going into Chrome whether the page in front of you is your bank or a forum.

Code editors and terminals are deliberately not on the list. Pasting a key into the file that needs it is the normal case there, and asking every time is how a warning becomes something you click through without reading.

On by default. To turn it off, or to change which applications it covers, run one of these in Terminal and restart Stashcut:

defaults write com.stashcut.app warnBeforeRiskyPaste -bool NO

defaults write com.stashcut.app riskyPasteBundleIdentifiers \
    "com.apple.Safari,com.google.Chrome,com.tinyspeck.slackmacgap"

The list is bundle identifiers separated by commas. An entry also covers anything extending it by a dot, so com.google.Chrome covers Chrome Canary and Chrome Beta without listing them. Using Don't Warn for ... writes the whole list into your preferences, which means applications added to the built-in list by a later version will not appear; defaults delete com.stashcut.app riskyPasteBundleIdentifiers puts the built-in list back.

When a clipping was copied

The top right of the bezel card says when you copied the clipping. Recent ones give their age: just now, 12 min ago, 2 h ago. Later the same day it is Today 09:12, the day before Yesterday 21:40, and anything older the full date with the year, 23 Sep 2026 12:33, so an old clipping is never mistaken for this year's. The clock is 24-hour; to see 9:40 PM instead, tick Show times with AM and PM in Settings > Appearance.

Images, and reading the text in them

Copy a screenshot and Stashcut keeps it like any other clipping. It reads the text out of the image first, on this Mac, with Apple's Vision framework: no model, no network, no Ollama. That recognised text is the clipping, so the screenshot shows up in the menu and the bezel by what it says, substring search finds it, and related search indexes it alongside everything else.

An image with no readable text is kept too, described by its size, for example Image, 1280 by 720, so it is still something you can find and paste.

In the bezel a screenshot shows itself: the picture on the card, with a Screenshot tag, and a small toolbar beside the counter. t (or the toolbar) turns the card over to the text read out of it, and back. z (or the toolbar's last button) shows it full screen; there t turns it over too, the text is large and you can select and copy part of it, the arrows move to the clipping before or after, ↩ pastes, and esc brings you back to the bezel exactly where you were. In the menu a screenshot has a small preview with a camera badge.

Pasting pastes what you see: the picture, or, turned over, the text. Never both at once, which some apps, web chats among them, would take as two pastes. In the menu, hold ⌥ over a screenshot to get Paste text instead.

The pictures live in ~/Library/Application Support/Stashcut/Clipboard Images. Only the file name is kept in your history, not the picture itself. Stashcut deletes any picture no clipping refers to when it starts, and keeps the folder under about 200 MB by dropping the oldest first. If a picture has gone, pasting that clipping still gives you its text.

A screenshot goes through the same credential check as copied text, so a picture of a terminal with an API key in it is kept hidden exactly as the key itself would be, its picture in memory only. Text recognition needs macOS 10.15 or later; on anything older the screenshot is still kept, described by its size.

To turn image capture off, run this in Terminal and restart Stashcut:

defaults write com.stashcut.app captureImages -bool NO

Links

Stashcut finds links by rule, not with a model: anything that starts with http://, https:// or www. and has a real host. A file name such as setup.py is not a link, nor is an email address, and nothing but an http or https link is ever offered to your browser.

A clipping that is a link is drawn as a link card: whose it is, large, on top, which is the part to check before opening anything, then the whole link with its parts in their own colours. Links inside other text are coloured and underlined where they are. In the menu, a link shows its domain in bold.

Copying a link sends nothing anywhere. Stashcut never looks a link up: no preview, no icon, no check of where it leads. It is opened only when you ask: press b in the bezel, click Open on the card, or hold ⌥ over the link in the menu and choose Open in browser. When a clipping holds more than one link, none is opened, because which one you meant would be a guess.

Finding a clipping by what it meant

Press ⇧+⌘+? to open the search window. Typing filters your history by substring as it always has. With semantic search on, it also lists clippings that are about what you typed even when they share no words with it, marked with ≈ and placed under the exact matches. Searching "pastry ingredients" will turn up "Recipe: 200g flour, 2 eggs, 100ml milk" that way.

Search by meaning uses an embedding model, which is different from the assistant's chat model. The bundled local search model needs no Ollama server or network connection. Settings > AI offers other search engines and optional model downloads. The first index can take a few seconds; afterwards only new clippings need indexing. Literal search remains available while the model is loading or turned off.

One vector per clipping is kept in ~/Library/Application Support/Stashcut/embeddings.plist. Bundled local search processes text on your Mac. If you configure a remote embedding endpoint, text is sent to that endpoint. Changing the embedding model rebuilds the index, because vectors made by different models cannot be compared.

Clippings shorter than two words, such as a licence plate, are skipped for related search: there is not enough text for the meaning to be measurable, and substring search finds them exactly anyway.

AI Actions

Assistant features are off until you turn them on in Settings > AI. Actions and Ask Your Clipboard use the model endpoint you choose. With Ollama, LM Studio or llama.cpp running locally, processing stays on your Mac. A remote endpoint receives the text needed for your request. Stashcut does not route your clipboard through its own servers.

Install Ollama and pull a model, for example ollama pull llama3.2. Then open Preferences > AI, tick Enable AI features, and press Refresh to list the models installed on this Mac. The status line tells you whether the endpoint was reachable and how many models it found.

If you never pick one, Stashcut chooses the smallest chat model you have installed the first time AI is actually used, and writes that choice into Preferences > AI so you can see and change it. Smallest, because it is the one that answers soonest, and a first run should not land on a 17 GB model by accident. A model you have chosen yourself is never replaced.

Once enabled, the menu gains an AI Actions submenu: Summarize, Rewrite Clearly, Fix Spelling & Grammar, Explain, and Translate to English. Each one acts on whatever is currently on the clipboard, so copy something (or pick a clipping from the menu first), then choose an action. The result is placed on the clipboard and kept as a layer of that clipping's stack, alongside its original.

In the bezel, a second row of keys under the first lists the AI actions that fit the card you are looking at, with their keys: all five for ordinary text and a screenshot's text, Summarize and Explain for code, none for a password, a hidden clipping or a bare link. While the bezel is open those keys act on that card rather than on the clipboard, and the card itself does the work: a cyan tag at the top of it names what is happening (Summarizing…, Fixing spelling…), three dots pulse beside it, and the clipping sits back until the answer starts to write itself in its place. When complete, the answer comes to the front of the same stack, named Summary, Explanation, Rewritten, Fixed or Translated. Exposed card edges and a layer counter make its depth visible, even on Original and on neighbouring cards. Return pastes whichever layer is at the front.

Left/Right or H/L change stacks. Up/Down or K/J change layers. A stack stays stacked when you leave it, and returning brings back the layer you last chose. Closing the bezel keeps the stack too. With history saving enabled, its layers and front selection also survive restarting Stashcut.

There is one slot per action: running Summarize again replaces Summary, leaving the original and other results intact. An action on a result uses that front layer as its input and keeps its answer in the same stack. A stack can contain the original plus five results, never duplicate summaries.

The defaults are ⇧+⌘+s Summarize, ⇧+⌘+r Rewrite, ⇧+⌘+f Fix Spelling & Grammar, ⇧+⌘+e Explain and ⇧+⌘+t Translate. Change them in Settings > Hotkeys. The AI row follows Show the row of keys under the bezel and only lists actions whose keys are set.

A small ellipsis appears in the menu bar while a request is running. From the menu, or from a Shift Command key with the bezel closed, the bezel comes up on the clipboard's clipping and the same thing happens there: the card is the one place an action is watched. It does not take focus, so you can keep typing where you were. Nothing is added to your history until the answer is finished.

Press Escape while the card is working, or Cancel AI Action in the AI Actions submenu, to stop a running action; the card is itself again, nothing is stored and nothing is said about it. If an action fails, the tag turns raspberry and one line beside it says why; Escape puts it away. Local models are not instant: the first run after a pause pays for loading the weights, and a large model can take a minute. Smaller models are listed first in Preferences > AI with their size, and one under about 5 GB usually answers in a few seconds.

If a request has run for twenty seconds with nothing to show yet, the card adds a sentence beside the tag saying why, either that the model is large or that it is reasoning before it answers, and points at the smaller models in Preferences > AI. It is said once and disappears the moment the answer starts arriving.

Reasoning models

A reasoning model, such as qwen3, thinks in writing before it answers. Those thinking tokens are most of the wait and none of the answer, so Stashcut asks such a model not to produce them for AI Actions and for Ask Your Clipboard: both are short transforms or retrieval-grounded answers that gain nothing from deliberation. Measured here on a warm qwen3.8, a Summarize went from 15.2 seconds before the first visible word to 1.8, and from 22.9 seconds in total to 8.6. An Ask Your Clipboard question over a 99-clipping history went from 65.4 seconds to the first words and 68.4 in total, to 0.3 and 3.3.

Stashcut only asks this of a model that says it can think, so nothing changes for a model that cannot, and if the runtime objects to the request Stashcut repeats it without and remembers not to ask that model again.

To have the reasoning instead of the speed:

defaults write com.stashcut.app aiAllowThinking -bool YES

There is no checkbox for it. With it on, and on any model whose runtime rejected the request, the thinking runs along the tag's row on the card as it streams, small and slanted and read from its end, so it cannot be mistaken for the answer. It is only something to watch while you wait: it is never stored as a clipping, never put on the clipboard, and never copied by Copy Answer.

Ask Your Clipboard

The last item in the AI Actions submenu opens a question box over your whole history. Ask something in plain language and Stashcut looks for the clippings that bear on it by meaning, gives those to the model, and writes an answer as the model produces it.

Under the answer is the list of clippings the question actually turned up, with a tick beside each one the answer drew on and a number matching the numbers in brackets in the answer itself. Click any of them to copy that clipping. The list is there so you can check the answer against what it was built from rather than take it on trust, which matters: a small local model will occasionally cite the wrong number or embellish, even when the right clippings were found.

When nothing in your history is related to the question, it says so and stops. It does not answer from whatever happened to score highest, because an answer assembled from unrelated clippings is confidently wrong and there is no way to tell from looking at it.

This needs both the AI switch and related-clipping search switched on in Preferences > AI, and it needs your clippings indexed. If they are not, the window indexes them first and then answers, so there is nothing to set up elsewhere.

Clipping text is sent only to the endpoint configured in Preferences. Point it at something other than this machine and that is where your clipboard text will go.
